$30 million fine leaves Ameritech customer service cold in December
Inside Technology

By Jon Van
Tribune Staff Writer
January 15, 2001

It may not be the biggest question as we enter the new millennium, but it's certainly a nagging one: Will Ameritech supply phone service reliably or are we in for a repeat of last year's nightmare?

While Ameritech's management promises to win back customer confidence in the coming year, there are reasons for concern.

Last week, for instance, a report to the Illinois Commerce Commission showed that Ameritech failed the state's basic service requirements for the month of December. The state requires that Ameritech restore service to 95 percent of its customers who lose use of phones within 24 hours.

Because it fell far short of that goal last year, Ameritech must pay a $30 million fine, which will come next month in the form of $5 rebates on February phone bills. But management had pledged that by year's end, service would be restored to acceptable levels.

"We knew that Ameritech was going to miss its service goals for the year," said Richard Mathias, the ICC chairman. "But they said service would be at acceptable levels by January. I thought that meant they'd be in compliance with service standards for December, and they weren't."

Ameritech was hampered by December's snows, said spokesman Dave Pacholczyk, and it regrets missing the target.

"We were in compliance until the weather hit us," he said. "We ended the month at 93 percent instead of 95 percent and we agree with Chairman Mathias that we must do better, and we will."

Another straw in the wind is a lawsuit filed last week against Ameritech by the Oak Mill Medical Practice of Niles. Oak Mill is a new practice with five physicians who care for about 7,000 patients.

The doctors have been planning to start their practice for several months and contacted Ameritech about installing phone lines in September. Although they ordered 14 phone lines, to be installed in December before the new offices were open, only one line was in place when they opened the first of this month. That line is always busy and frustrated patients couldn't get through.

Seeing no alternative to get Ameritech's attention, the physicians filed a lawsuit in Cook County Circuit Court seeking a court order to make Ameritech install those lines. Within a few days after the suit was filed, Ameritech installed the extra lines, Pacholczyk said.

"We had problems because we couldn't get lines through existing conduit," said Pacholczyk. "It took extra time for us to determine an alternative to get additional lines to the building, but they have them now."

Groupware coup? Despite gloomy times generally for dot-com firms, it's hard to keep a techie down once a good idea is hatched, as has happened with Objectsoft, a Chicago-based computer applications consultancy.

To keep the firm's consultants in touch with one another while they're on the road, Objectsoft devised its own groupware that enables users to quickly e-mail each other, arrange for meetings, keep track of tasks and so on. It's something like a Lotus Notes designed to access Web-enabled mobile phones, Palm Pilots, personal computers, laptops and the like.

The groupware worked so well that last year Objectsoft started a sister company, NotWired, to market it to others. The firm is targeting groups of professionals such as lawyers, consultants and real estate sales people who carry wireless devices and need to keep in touch.

So far, NotWired has lined up about 200 clients, said Kevin Clark, chief executive for Objectsoft and NotWired. Business applications seem most likely to drive the wireless Web in the near future, Clark said.

"Have you ever tried looking up a restaurant on a Web phone?" asked Clark. "It's not that easy. You can get movie information, weather and some news, but that's about all. And how many times a week do you need to know about movies?

"In my opinion, business applications are going to be used more than consumer ones right now. You do need to get e-mails and know your schedule and what tasks you're supposed to do. As wireless Web connections get faster and screens get bigger, this will catch on. But right now, business needs will drive this."

Cold cement: A Purdue University doctoral student has led a team effort to develop a new cement mix that cures in subfreezing temperatures.

The first application of the new cement is at a nuclear power plant run by the Tennessee Valley Authority, but the mix could have a major impact on the construction industry, which spends hundreds of millions a year to heat construction sites where concrete is poured.

The TVA became interested in subfreezing cement because it needed to put new floors into a nuclear plant's ice storage room. Raising the temperature in the room so a new floor could cure properly would require shutting down the plant, at a cost of about $1 million a day.

As an alternative, a task force headed by Charles Korhonen devised a lightweight Portland cement mix that could be used in concrete, enabling it to be poured and cured at temperatures around 20 degrees Fahrenheit. Korhonen, a research civil engineer with the U.S. Army's cold regions research and engineering laboratory, is working with low-temperature cement as part of his doctoral thesis at Purdue.

"This technology for placing concrete at subfreezing temperatures could extend the concrete construction season by several months in much of North America," Korhonen said.
